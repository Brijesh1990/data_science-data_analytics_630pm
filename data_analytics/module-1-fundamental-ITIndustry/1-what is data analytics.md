# What Is Data Analytics?

**Data analytics** is the process of collecting, organizing, cleaning, and examining data to discover useful information, answer questions, identify patterns, and support better decisions.

For example, a shop can analyze its sales data to find its best-selling products, understand seasonal changes, and decide how much stock to order.

## Common Steps in Data Analytics

1. **Define the question** to be answered.
2. **Collect** relevant data.
3. **Clean and prepare** the data by correcting errors and handling missing values.
4. **Analyze** the data using calculations, statistics, or software.
5. **Communicate** the findings with reports, charts, or dashboards.
6. **Take action** based on the results and evaluate the outcome.

## Main Types of Data

There is no single list of data types that covers every purpose. Data can be classified in several ways, and one dataset can belong to multiple categories at once.

### 1. By Value or Measurement

#### Qualitative (Categorical) Data

Qualitative data describes qualities, labels, or groups. It is usually expressed as words or categories rather than measured amounts.

- **Nominal data:** Categories with no natural order. Examples: eye color, country, or product category.
- **Ordinal data:** Categories that have a meaningful order, but where the gaps between categories are not necessarily equal. Examples: small/medium/large, satisfaction ratings, or class rank.

#### Quantitative (Numerical) Data

Quantitative data represents counts or measurements and can be expressed as numbers.

- **Discrete data:** Countable values, usually whole numbers. Examples: number of orders, students, or website visits.
- **Continuous data:** Measurements that can take any value within a range, including decimals. Examples: height, temperature, time, or distance.

### 2. By Level of Measurement

These four levels describe what comparisons and calculations are meaningful:

- **Nominal:** Labels only; no ranking (for example, blood group).
- **Ordinal:** Ordered categories; the size of the difference between ranks is not defined (for example, a one-to-five-star rating).
- **Interval:** Ordered numeric values with equal intervals but no true zero (for example, temperature in Celsius).
- **Ratio:** Ordered numeric values with equal intervals and a meaningful zero (for example, age, weight, or income).

### 3. By Organization or Format

- **Structured data:** Organized in a fixed format, such as rows and columns in a spreadsheet or database. Examples: customer IDs, dates, and transaction amounts.
- **Semi-structured data:** Has some organization, but not a strict table structure. Examples: JSON, XML, and many log files.
- **Unstructured data:** Does not follow a predefined tabular format. Examples: documents, emails, images, audio, and video.

### 4. By Time and Observation

- **Cross-sectional data:** Information about many subjects at one point or period in time. Example: a survey of customers this month.
- **Time-series data:** Observations recorded over time, often at regular intervals. Example: daily sales over a year.
- **Panel (longitudinal) data:** Repeated observations of the same subjects over time. Example: annual spending records for the same group of customers.

### 5. By Source

- **Primary data:** Collected directly for a particular purpose, such as through an interview, survey, experiment, or observation.
- **Secondary data:** Previously collected by someone else or for another purpose, such as a public census, research report, or company archive.

## Four Common Types of Data Analytics

These describe the questions analysts ask; they are **not** types of data:

1. **Descriptive analytics — What happened?** Summarizes past data, such as monthly revenue.
2. **Diagnostic analytics — Why did it happen?** Investigates causes, such as why sales declined.
3. **Predictive analytics — What may happen next?** Uses patterns and models to estimate future outcomes.
4. **Prescriptive analytics — What should we do?** Recommends actions, such as how to allocate inventory.

## Why Data Analytics Is Useful

Data analytics can help people and organizations:

- Make evidence-based decisions.
- Find trends, problems, and opportunities.
- Understand customers and improve services.
- Measure performance and plan for the future.

**In short:** data is the information being examined; data analytics is the process of turning that information into useful findings and decisions.
